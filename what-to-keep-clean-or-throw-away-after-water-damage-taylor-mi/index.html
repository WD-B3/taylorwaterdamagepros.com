What to Keep, Clean or Throw Away After Water Damage: A Taylor, MI Guide

Sorting a wet house is hard on a bad day. This framework makes the choices quicker and keeps your claim safe.

By the Taylor Water Damage Pros team

☎ (734) 865-2522

After water damage, the question that comes up fastest is what to throw away. The answer depends on two things: how contaminated the water was and how porous the item is. Clean water is gentler than dirty water, and hard items are easier to save than soft ones. Before you discard anything, a record matters for your claim.

First rule: document before you discard

Michigan's insurance regulator advises taking photos or videos of damaged items and not discarding them until your insurer instructs you to. Make a list as you go, with a description, age and approximate value. If the item is a health hazard, such as spoiled food or sewage-soaked material, photograph it and discard it, since safety comes first. Ask your insurer how to handle such items.

Sorting by material

The categories of water are explained in detail on the flood damage page and the sewage backup page. The EPA's guidance on clean-water damage gives specific rules for porous materials. Ceiling tiles that got wet should be discarded and replaced, and wallboard can be dried in place only if it shows no obvious swelling and its seams are intact.

Cleaning what can be saved

  • Wipe hard items with a detergent solution, then dry them fully.
  • Wash fabrics on the hottest setting that the label allows.
  • Dry wood furniture slowly, away from direct heat that could warp it.
  • Leave items that smell or show growth to a professional cleaner.
  • Set aside valuable or sentimental items for expert advice.

Dry quickly. The longer items stay wet, the more likely they are past saving. The 24 to 48 hour window in EPA guidance applies to materials in the house, and it is a guideline rather than a guarantee. Keep wet items out of the drying area if they are not part of the dry-out, so they do not add moisture to the room.

Photos, papers and electronics

Wet photographs and papers have their own steps, covered in a separate guide on saving photos and documents. Electronics should not be turned on until they are checked. For both, speed and gentle handling matter more than scrubbing.

Working through a room

  1. Make the room safe and stop any source of water.
  2. Photograph the room as you found it, wide shots first and then close-ups.
  3. Move items to a dry area in three piles: keep, clean and discard.
  4. Label anything you set aside for your insurer so you can find it later.
  5. Wait for your insurer before you haul away large items.

Work from the least damaged items to the most. Hard goods go first because they are quick to wipe down. Porous goods such as cushions and cardboard boxes need a decision quickly, since they hold water and can spread it to everything near them.

Look after yourself

Sorting a wet house is physical and emotional. Wear gloves and sturdy shoes, take breaks and ask a friend to help you carry. Some items matter more than they cost. Set those aside, and let the rest go without guilt when the facts say they cannot be saved.

Special cases

  • Medicine and cosmetics that touched water should be discarded.
  • Stuffed toys can often be washed if the water was clean.
  • Books and papers follow separate steps, which depend on how soon you can dry them.
  • Small appliances that were wet should be checked by a technician before use.
  • Antiques and family heirlooms deserve an expert opinion before you clean them.

When you are not sure, set the item aside in a dry place and photograph it. A day of waiting rarely changes the answer, while an item thrown away too soon cannot be added to a claim.

Taylor, MI: matching what you lose with what your policy says

Taylor's median owner-occupied home value is $152,600, and many owners hold coverage for contents as well as the structure. A policy may value contents at replacement cost or at a depreciated amount, so ask your insurer which applies. A good inventory, built while the items are in front of you, makes that conversation easier.

The state regulator also notes that water or sewer backup coverage is usually a limited endorsement, so contents lost to a backup may have separate limits. Sources used: U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts for Taylor and Michigan DIFS guidance.

Questions people ask

Should I throw away a couch that got wet?

It depends on the water. Clean water that is dried quickly can leave a couch saved. Contaminated water usually means discarding upholstered pieces.

Do I have to keep damaged items for my insurer in Taylor?

Michigan's regulator advises keeping them until your insurer says they can be discarded, and photographing them.

What about food that touched water?

Discard it. Do not take chances with food or medicine that the water reached.

Can I keep a mattress that got a little wet?

It is hard to dry deep layers. Most are discarded, but ask your insurer and a professional.
