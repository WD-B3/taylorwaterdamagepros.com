How to Save Photos and Important Documents After Water Damage: Taylor, MI

Wet paper can often be saved if you act inside about two days. The steps follow the National Archives.

By the Taylor Water Damage Pros team

☎ (734) 865-2522

Photos, birth certificates, deeds and letters are what people grieve over after a flood. Many can be saved, but the window is short, and some well-meant steps do harm. This guide follows the advice of the National Archives and Records Administration for wet family papers and photographs.

The two-day rule

The National Archives advises that wet books, documents or photographs that cannot be air dried quickly should be frozen to inhibit mold growth. It sets about two days as the point to decide. Freezing does not kill mold, it only makes it dormant until you can treat the items. The one exception is old photographs and negatives, which the Archives says should never be frozen.

ItemWhat the National Archives advises
Wet documentsAir dry flat, or freeze if you cannot dry them within about two days
Modern photographsDry individually, face up, on clean blotting material
Old photographs and negativesDo not freeze. Dry carefully and consult a conservator
Valuable or irreplaceable itemsConsult a conservator before any treatment

Handle them gently

  • Wet paper is very fragile, so lift it carefully and support it from below.
  • Wear rubber gloves if the papers have contaminated mud or silt on them.
  • If an item is wet and dirty, swish it in clear water to remove grime. Do not rinse images that are blurred or faded.
  • Replace blotting material when it soaks, and use unprinted paper or clean cloth.
  • Place fans for air circulation, not aimed directly at the items.

Do not use a hair dryer or an oven, and do not press wet items against each other. A clean, flat surface in a part of the house away from the dry-out is the best place to work.

If the water was sewage or flood water, treat the papers as contaminated and wear gloves, and ask a professional about whether to try to save them. The cleanup for such water is on the sewage backup page.

Why speed matters

Wet paper left in a warm, damp room grows mold quickly. Freezing buys time, but the Archives is clear that freezing does not kill mold, it only holds it still until you can dry the items properly. The goal is to get items either dry or frozen inside the two-day window, and to keep the room they sit in dry.

Call a conservator for valuable items

For items of high monetary, historic or sentimental value, the Archives recommends treatment only in consultation with a conservator. A rare print with no negative should go to one immediately. A professional cannot always save everything, but an expert opinion is better than a guess.

Set up a drying station

Pick a dry room away from the damage and cover a table with clean towels or unprinted paper. Lay papers and photos flat in a single layer without overlap. Replace the blotting material as it soaks up water. Keep the room cool, let air move gently through it and leave the items undisturbed as they dry.

Keep a short log of what you set out and when. If you freeze items, write the contents on the bag and keep the bags flat. Sort the most valuable pieces first, because those are the ones to show a conservator if they need expert help.

Digital backups afterward

Once photos are dry, scan them at a good resolution and store copies in two places, such as a drive at home and a cloud account. Do the same for deeds, tax papers, insurance policies and identification. The next time water arrives, the paper may be lost, but the copy will not be.

If the pile is large, do not try to save everything at once. Triage by value and replaceability. Birth certificates, deeds and one-of-a-kind photographs come first, and routine bills and flyers last. Ask a friend to help with the sorting, since many hands make the two-day window far easier to meet.

Do not use tape, paper clips or sticky notes on wet items, and do not peel apart papers that have stuck together. Leave stuck sheets as they are and ask a conservator what to do. Pulling them apart is how many damaged pages are lost for good.

Taylor, MI: copies, replacements and record keeping

Some documents can be replaced. For Michigan birth, death and marriage records, ask the county clerk or the state vital records office how to request copies, and ask your bank or title company about deeds and policies. Keep a list of what was damaged and what you replaced, since your insurer may want it.

The state insurance regulator advises photographing damaged items and keeping them until the insurer says they can go. That includes papers you could not save. Sources used: National Archives and Records Administration guidance on flood damage to family papers, and Michigan DIFS guidance.

Dry the room too

Papers in a wet room dry badly. Crews serving Taylor, Southgate and Dearborn can dry the room while you work on the items. The structural drying page explains how.

Questions people ask

Should I freeze old photographs?

No. The National Archives says never to freeze old photographs or negatives. Dry them carefully and consult a conservator for valuable ones.

How long do I have to save wet papers?

About two days is the point where items that cannot be air dried should be frozen to stop mold, according to the National Archives.

Where can a Taylor resident request replacement vital records?

Start with the county clerk or the state vital records office, and ask what documents they need.

Is it safe to handle moldy papers?

Wear gloves and dry or freeze the items quickly. Freezing does not kill mold, and valuable items should go to a conservator.
